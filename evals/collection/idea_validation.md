# Idea Validation

Validating a startup idea means finding evidence that a specific group of people has a problem painful enough that they will change their behavior to solve it. Validation is not about confirming your idea is good. It is about trying to find out quickly whether it is bad, so you stop spending time on it.



## Problem/solution fit versus product/market fit

These two milestones are often confused. Problem/solution fit means you have confirmed that a real, recurring, painful problem exists and that your proposed approach plausibly addresses it. You can reach problem/solution fit before writing any code. Product/market fit means you have shipped something and the market is pulling it out of your hands: usage grows without heavy promotion, churn is low, and customers complain when the product breaks.

One common quantitative proxy for product/market fit is the Sean Ellis test. Survey your active users and ask how they would feel if they could no longer use the product. If at least 40 percent answer "very disappointed," that is generally treated as a signal you have found product/market fit. Below roughly 25 percent, you almost certainly have not.



## Customer interviews

The most reliable early validation tool is a direct conversation with someone who has the problem. The core discipline, popularized in Rob Fitzpatrick's book *The Mom Test*, is to ask about the person's life and past behavior rather than about your idea. Your mother will tell you your idea is great; a stranger will too, if you ask badly.

Good questions are backward-looking and specific:

- "Walk me through the last time this happened."
- "What did you do about it?"
- "What did that cost you, in time or money?"
- "What else have you tried? Why did you stop?"

Bad questions are forward-looking and hypothetical: "Would you use a tool that did X?" or "Do you think this is a good idea?" People are poor predictors of their own future behavior and are socially motivated to be encouraging.

Aim for at least 10 to 20 interviews with people in a single, narrowly defined segment before drawing conclusions. Fewer than that and you are pattern-matching on noise. Interviewing across many unrelated segments produces contradictory feedback that is impossible to act on.



## Signals of real demand

Real signals involve the person giving up something scarce. In rough order of increasing strength:

- They describe a problem in vivid, unprompted detail, including what it costs them.
- They have already built a workaround: a spreadsheet, a manual process, a duct-taped set of tools.
- They are currently paying for something inadequate.
- They agree to a follow-up meeting and show up.
- They introduce you to a colleague with the same problem.
- They sign a letter of intent, join a paid pilot, or pre-order.

Money and time are the two currencies that matter. A prospect who pays even a small amount, or who commits several hours to a pilot, has given you real information.



## Common false positives

These feel like validation and are not:

- **Compliments.** "This is really cool" and "I love this" carry no predictive weight. Compliments are the sound of a conversation going nowhere.
- **Enthusiastic hypotheticals.** "I would absolutely use that" is a statement about the speaker's self-image, not their future behavior.
- **Feature requests without commitment.** A long wish list from someone who will not commit to buying is a distraction. Ask what they would pay for it today; the answer is usually revealing.
- **Waitlist signups.** An email address costs nothing to give. Waitlists measure the appeal of your landing page copy, not demand for your product.
- **Friends and family.** Their feedback is contaminated by their relationship with you.
- **Vanity survey results.** Surveys asking people to rate ideas on a 1-to-5 scale reliably produce middling positive scores regardless of the idea.



## Practical sequence

A workable early sequence looks like this. Define one narrow customer segment. Conduct 10 to 20 interviews focused entirely on past behavior. Identify whether a workaround exists and what it costs. If a painful, expensive, frequent problem shows up repeatedly, propose a specific solution and try to get a small commitment before building: a paid pilot, a design partnership, a signed letter of intent. Only then build the smallest thing that tests whether your solution actually removes the pain.

If you cannot get anyone to commit anything before you build, that is itself a finding. It usually means the problem is real but not urgent, and a product built for it will struggle to get attention even when it works.
