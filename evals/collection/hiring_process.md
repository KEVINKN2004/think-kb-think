# Hiring Process

Early hiring decisions have outsized consequences because each person represents a large fraction of the company and sets precedent for everyone who follows. A ten-person company adding one hire is changing 10 percent of its culture.



## Hire generalists first

The first ten hires should be generalists who can operate without structure. An early-stage company cannot yet describe the job precisely, priorities shift monthly, and there is no process to lean on. Specialists thrive inside defined systems and often struggle in their absence.

The practical test is whether a candidate has previously done work outside their formal role and enjoyed it. An engineer who has talked to customers, or a marketer who has built their own landing pages, is more likely to succeed than a highly credentialed specialist accustomed to a support structure that does not exist yet.

Specialists become correct hires once a function is large enough to be a full-time job with predictable work — usually when a generalist is already doing that work badly for lack of time.



## Do not hire managers early

A common and expensive mistake is hiring managers before there is anyone to manage. A director or VP hired into a five-person company typically has no team, no budget, and no processes to run, and their skill set — coordinating and developing others — has no application. They also compress the growth path for early individual contributors.

Hire managers when a team has grown to the point where coordination genuinely consumes someone's full attention, commonly around five to seven direct reports doing similar work. Before then, founders should manage directly.



## Contractor versus employee

The distinction is worth deliberate thought rather than defaulting to full-time hiring.

**Contractors** fit work that is well-defined, bounded in scope, specialized, and not core to the company's long-term differentiation. Design for a specific launch, a legal review, bookkeeping, a one-off data migration, or a marketing campaign are typical. Contractors cost more per hour but carry no long-term commitment, no equity, and minimal onboarding overhead.

**Employees** fit work that is ongoing, requires accumulating context, and touches the core of the product or business. Anything where institutional knowledge compounds — core engineering, customer relationships, product decisions — belongs in-house.

Two cautions. First, classification is a legal matter, not a preference: misclassifying someone who works full-time hours under your direction and control as a contractor creates real liability in most jurisdictions, and the tests vary by location. Second, a contract-to-hire trial can be an excellent way to evaluate fit, but it should be paid, time-boxed, and explicit about the possibility of conversion.



## Paid work trials

The most reliable early evaluation method is a small, paid project resembling actual work — commonly 4 to 8 hours, always compensated at a fair rate. This surfaces how someone communicates, handles ambiguity, and responds to feedback, none of which interviews reveal well.

Unpaid take-home assignments of significant length are both unfair and a filter for candidates with free time rather than candidates with talent. Keeping trials paid and short protects candidate goodwill, which matters when your company is unknown.



## Early employee equity

Early employees take real risk and should hold meaningful equity. Approximate ranges for a company at seed stage:

- **First employee** (very early, pre-product-market-fit): roughly 1 to 2 percent
- **Early senior engineer or first functional lead**: roughly 0.5 to 1.5 percent
- **Employees roughly 10 through 20**: commonly 0.1 to 0.5 percent
- **Executive hires** (VP or C-level, post-Series A): often 0.5 to 2 percent depending on level and stage

These figures fall as the company de-risks; an employee joining at Series B accepts less equity because their risk is lower and the shares are worth more. Grants normally follow the same four-year vesting with one-year cliff structure used for founders.

Be transparent about what the equity represents. Give the number of shares, total shares outstanding, the strike price, and the most recent valuation. A percentage without the denominator is meaningless, and candidates who have been burned before will ask.



## Hiring pace after a raise

A predictable failure pattern follows a fundraise: the company hires quickly because it now has money, headcount grows ahead of revenue, coordination overhead rises, and the extended runway the raise was meant to buy evaporates.

Hiring should be paced against demonstrated need — a bottleneck that is measurably constraining the business — rather than against available cash. Each hire also carries an onboarding cost in existing employees' time, so hiring several people simultaneously into a small team can temporarily reduce total output.



## Early culture

Culture in a small company is not values written on a wall. It is the behavior that gets rewarded, tolerated, and promoted, and it is set almost entirely by what the founders actually do.

Three mechanisms matter more than statements. First, who you hire: each person's habits become the norm. Second, who you promote and praise, which signals what the company genuinely values regardless of stated principles. Third, who you tolerate: keeping a high performer who treats colleagues badly communicates, unambiguously, that results excuse behavior.

The highest-leverage early culture decision is usually the first firing. Removing someone who is producing but corrosive is difficult and often delayed, and the delay itself teaches everyone what the real standard is.
