# Co-founder Decisions

Choosing a co-founder and structuring the relationship is among the highest-consequence decisions a founder makes, and one of the least reversible. Research by Noam Wasserman, published in *The Founder's Dilemmas*, found that roughly 65 percent of startup failures involve co-founder conflict rather than product or market failure. The mechanics below exist to reduce that risk.



## When to bring on a co-founder

A co-founder is warranted when the work genuinely requires two people operating at an owner's level of commitment and judgment — typically when the business needs both deep technical execution and deep customer-facing work, and no single person can carry both. A co-founder is not warranted merely because you want company, because an accelerator prefers teams, or because you need a specific skill for a few months. The latter case calls for a contractor or an early employee.

The test worth applying: would you want this person making unilateral decisions about the company while you were unreachable for two weeks? If not, they should not have founder-level equity and control.



## Equity splits

Two broad approaches exist.

**Equal splits** (50/50 for two founders, or roughly equal for more) are simple, signal mutual respect, and avoid ongoing resentment about a few percentage points. They are common and often correct when both founders join at the same time with comparable commitment.

**Unequal splits** reflect real differences in contribution: who had the idea, who has been working on it longer, who is taking a larger pay cut, who is full-time versus part-time, who is contributing capital, and who holds relevant domain expertise or relationships. A 60/40 or 70/30 split is unremarkable when the underlying asymmetry is real.

The most important consideration is not the exact ratio but whether both parties believe it is fair after honest discussion. A split that one founder privately resents will surface later, usually at the worst possible moment.

Avoid deferring the conversation. "We'll figure out equity later" is a common and expensive mistake, because the longer it waits, the more each founder has accumulated a private sense of their own relative contribution.



## Vesting

Vesting means founders earn their shares over time rather than owning them outright on day one. Unvested shares are forfeited if the founder leaves.

The market standard is **four-year vesting with a one-year cliff**. Under this structure, a founder who leaves before completing 12 months receives nothing. At the 12-month mark, 25 percent vests at once. The remaining 75 percent then vests monthly over the following 36 months.

Vesting protects the company and the remaining founders from the scenario where someone leaves after three months and retains a large permanent stake, making future fundraising and hiring difficult. It protects the departing founder too, by establishing a clear, pre-agreed outcome rather than a negotiation during a difficult exit.

Founders sometimes resist vesting on their own shares, viewing it as distrust. The better framing is insurance: you are protecting the company from a bad outcome neither of you expects.

Investors will require founder vesting if it is not already in place, and will often reset the clock at the financing, so establishing it early preserves credit for time already served.



## Acceleration

Vesting agreements often specify what happens on acquisition.

**Single-trigger acceleration** means vesting accelerates on a change of control alone. Acquirers generally dislike this, because it removes the retention incentive they are partly paying for.

**Double-trigger acceleration** requires two events: a change of control *and* the founder being terminated without cause within a defined window afterward, commonly 12 months. Double-trigger is the more standard and more defensible arrangement.



## The 83(b) election

Founders who receive stock subject to vesting should file an 83(b) election with the IRS **within 30 days** of the grant. This election tells the tax authority to treat the entire grant as income at its current — usually negligible — value, rather than taxing each tranche as it vests at the then-current, potentially much higher, value.

The 30-day deadline is strict and there is no remedy for missing it. Failing to file can create substantial tax liability on paper gains long before any shares can be sold. This is one of the few startup formalities where a small administrative lapse produces a large, unfixable financial consequence.



## The written founder agreement

Before significant work begins, put the following in writing:

- Equity split and vesting schedule for each founder
- Roles, titles, and decision rights, including who breaks ties
- Expected time commitment, and whether anyone is part-time
- What happens if a founder leaves voluntarily, is asked to leave, or becomes unable to work
- Intellectual property assignment to the company
- How future equity decisions and the option pool will be handled

The value is less in the document's enforceability than in the conversation required to produce it. Disagreements surfaced during a calm drafting session are far cheaper than the same disagreements discovered during a crisis.
