# Market Sizing

Market sizing estimates how much revenue is theoretically available to a business. Founders use it to decide whether an opportunity is worth pursuing; investors use it to judge whether a company could plausibly become large. The number itself is always wrong. The reasoning behind it is what matters.



## TAM, SAM, and SOM

Three nested figures are standard.

**TAM (Total Addressable Market)** is the total annual revenue available if you captured 100 percent of demand for your category, globally, with no constraints on geography, competition, or capacity. It is the outermost boundary.

**SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market)** is the portion of TAM your product could actually serve given your current model — the relevant geography, language, regulatory environment, customer size, and product scope. If you sell only in North America to companies with 50 to 500 employees, your SAM reflects those limits.

**SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market)** is the share of SAM you could realistically win in a defined period, usually three to five years, given competition and your own capacity to sell and deliver. SOM is the figure most closely tied to an actual revenue plan.

A useful shorthand: TAM is the category, SAM is the segment you can serve, SOM is what you can win.



## Bottom-up sizing

Bottom-up sizing builds the number from unit economics. The basic form is:

**Number of potential customers × average annual revenue per customer = market size**

Suppose you sell scheduling software to independent dental practices in the United States. There are roughly 130,000 such practices. If your annual contract value is $2,400 per practice, your SAM is 130,000 × $2,400, or about $312 million.

Bottom-up is strongly preferred by investors because every input is inspectable. If someone disputes the number, they can argue with the customer count or the price, and you can defend each separately. It also forces you to know your pricing, which many founders have not settled.



## Top-down sizing

Top-down sizing starts from a published industry figure and narrows it with assumptions. For example: an analyst report says the global HR software market is $40 billion; you assume 10 percent applies to small businesses, giving $4 billion.

Top-down is faster but weaker. Its failure mode is the arbitrary percentage — "we only need 1 percent of a $50 billion market" is a well-known red flag in pitch meetings, because it asserts a result without explaining how any single customer is acquired. Use top-down only as a sanity check on a bottom-up figure, never as the primary estimate.



## What size investors look for

Venture capital math requires the possibility of very large outcomes, because most investments return nothing. A fund needs individual companies capable of returning the entire fund.

As a rough guide, venture investors typically want to believe the TAM can support a $1 billion or larger business, which in practice means a TAM of at least $1 billion and often several billion. A company addressing a $50 million market may be an excellent business but is generally not venture-fundable, because even total dominance would not produce a fund-returning outcome.

This is not a judgment about quality. Many profitable, durable companies serve small markets. It is a statement about the fit between a business and a particular financing model. Founders in genuinely small markets are usually better served by bootstrapping, revenue-based financing, or debt.



## Market growth and timing

Size alone is insufficient. Investors also weigh growth rate and timing. A $2 billion market growing 30 percent annually is more attractive than a $5 billion market shrinking 5 percent annually, because the former will be larger in five years and incumbents are less entrenched relative to new demand.

The related concept is "why now." Markets open when something changes: a regulation, a cost curve, a platform shift, a behavioral change. A credible market-sizing narrative explains not just how big the opportunity is but why it is available at this moment rather than five years ago.



## Common mistakes

- **Defining the market so broadly it becomes meaningless.** "We're in the $3 trillion healthcare market" tells a listener nothing.
- **Using TAM where SOM is the honest number.** Presenting a global category figure as your near-term opportunity damages credibility once the questions start.
- **Ignoring the budget question.** A problem can be widespread and still have no existing budget line to pay for a solution. Where the money currently comes from matters as much as how many people have the problem.
- **Counting customers who cannot buy.** Segments blocked by procurement, regulation, or long incumbent contracts belong outside SAM, not inside it.

The strongest presentation is a bottom-up SAM with visible inputs, a top-down cross-check, and an explicit statement of which assumption the whole figure is most sensitive to.
