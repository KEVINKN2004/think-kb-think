# Fundraising Stages

Startup financing proceeds through recognizable stages, each with rough conventions around amount raised, dilution, valuation, and what the company must demonstrate. The figures below are typical ranges, not rules; they vary with geography, sector, and market conditions.



## Pre-seed

Pre-seed capital funds the period before meaningful traction. Typical raise is **$500,000 to $2 million**. Sources are angel investors, pre-seed specialist funds, and accelerators.

At this stage investors are underwriting the team and the insight rather than metrics. A working prototype, early design partners, or evidence of unusual founder domain expertise are the common proof points. Revenue is not expected.

Pre-seed rounds are usually structured as convertible instruments rather than priced equity rounds, because negotiating a valuation is expensive and largely arbitrary this early.



## Seed

Seed capital funds the search for product/market fit. Typical raise is **$2 million to $5 million**, though seed rounds have grown and figures above this are increasingly common.

**Typical dilution in a seed round is 15 to 25 percent.** That is, founders and existing holders collectively give up roughly a fifth of the company. Dilution meaningfully above 25 percent at seed is worth scrutinizing, as it compounds through later rounds and can leave founders with too little ownership to stay motivated through a Series B or C.

Investors at seed expect a shipped product, early users, and some evidence of retention or willingness to pay. Hard revenue thresholds are uncommon, but a credible story about early traction is not optional.

Seed rounds may be priced or done on convertible instruments. Larger seeds are more often priced.



## Series A

Series A funds scaling a model that already works. Typical raise is **$10 million to $20 million**, with dilution again commonly in the 15 to 25 percent range.

Series A is where quantitative bars become real. A frequently cited benchmark for software companies is roughly **$1 million to $2 million in ARR** with strong growth, though this varies widely by sector and by how efficiently that revenue was acquired. Investors also examine retention, gross margin, sales efficiency, and whether growth comes from a repeatable channel rather than founder heroics.

The qualitative bar is repeatability. Seed asks "does anyone want this?" Series A asks "can you reliably acquire customers at a cost that makes sense?"



## Growth expectations

A widely referenced growth heuristic for enterprise software is **triple, triple, double, double, double** — tripling revenue in each of the first two years after finding product/market fit, then doubling for three years. A company following that path grows roughly 72x over five years. It is an aspirational benchmark used to frame what venture-scale growth looks like, not a requirement for a healthy business.



## Convertible instruments: SAFEs and notes

Early rounds often use instruments that convert to equity later rather than pricing the company immediately.

A **SAFE** (Simple Agreement for Future Equity), created by Y Combinator, gives the investor the right to receive equity at a future priced round. SAFEs are not debt: there is no interest and no maturity date.

A **convertible note** is debt that converts to equity. It carries an interest rate and a maturity date, which introduces the possibility of repayment demands if no round occurs.

Two key terms appear in both:

- **Valuation cap** — the maximum valuation at which the investment converts. A lower cap means the investor receives more equity. This is the term that most affects dilution.
- **Discount** — a percentage reduction off the next round's price, commonly 10 to 20 percent. Where both exist, the investor typically receives whichever is more favorable.

Since 2018 the standard YC form has been the **post-money SAFE**, which fixes the investor's ownership percentage after the SAFE money is included. This makes dilution far easier to calculate than under the earlier pre-money form.

A recurring hazard is **SAFE stacking**: raising a series of SAFEs at different caps without modeling their combined effect. Founders can discover at the priced round that converting instruments consume substantially more of the company than expected. Maintain a cap table model that shows post-conversion ownership before signing each new instrument.



## The option pool

Investors typically require an employee stock option pool, commonly **10 to 20 percent** of the company, to be available for future hires.

The critical detail is timing. If the pool is created *before* the investment closes — a "pre-money" pool, sometimes called the option pool shuffle — the dilution falls entirely on existing shareholders, meaning founders. If created after, it dilutes everyone including the new investors. This single structural choice can shift several percentage points of ownership and is worth negotiating explicitly.



## Dilution over time

Each round compounds. A founding team starting at 100 percent, giving up 20 percent at seed and 20 percent at Series A, retains roughly 64 percent before accounting for the option pool. After a pool and a Series B, founders commonly hold somewhere in the range of 40 to 50 percent collectively. Modeling this early informs how much to raise and at what valuation, since raising more than necessary is a permanent cost paid in ownership.
