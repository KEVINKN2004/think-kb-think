# Early Go-to-Market

Go-to-market covers how a company reaches customers and converts them. In the earliest stage the goal is not an efficient, scalable machine. It is to find any repeatable path to a paying customer, then make it less manual.



## Founder-led sales

For the first customers, founders should sell personally. This is not a resource constraint to be worked around; it is the point. Founders learn objections, language, and pricing sensitivity that no report conveys, and they can change the product in response.

A common mistake is hiring a salesperson too early. Before there is a repeatable pitch and evidence that the product closes deals, a salesperson has nothing to execute and will generally fail — costing months and damaging the founders' confidence in the channel rather than the process. The usual guidance is that founders should personally close somewhere in the range of the first 10 to 20 customers before hiring sales.

The related principle, articulated by Paul Graham as "do things that don't scale," is that manual, unscalable effort in the early stage is correct. Onboarding customers by hand, writing personalized emails, and delivering parts of the service manually all generate learning and goodwill that automated systems cannot.



## Defining the ideal customer profile

An **ICP (ideal customer profile)** is a specific description of the customer for whom your product is most obviously valuable. It typically specifies company size, industry, role of the buyer, the trigger that creates urgency, and what they currently use instead.

The most common early-stage GTM error is defining the ICP too broadly. "Small and medium businesses" is not an ICP. "Dental practices with 2 to 5 locations that currently coordinate scheduling across offices by phone" is. A narrow ICP makes messaging sharper, referrals more likely, and outbound lists actually buildable.

Narrowing feels like giving up market, which is why founders resist it. In practice a narrow initial segment is the fastest route to a wider one, because a product that clearly wins for one group produces reference customers, case studies, and word of mouth.



## Positioning

Positioning is the answer to "what is this, who is it for, and why is it better than the alternative for them." The alternative is frequently not a competitor but the status quo: a spreadsheet, a manual process, or doing nothing.

A workable structure for early positioning is: for [specific customer], who [has this problem], our product is a [category] that [key benefit], unlike [current alternative], because [differentiator].

The category you claim matters, because it determines what the buyer compares you to and what they expect to pay. Positioning a product as a "spreadsheet replacement" invites a different price expectation than positioning it as a "compliance platform," even if the software is identical.



## Early channels

A handful of channels are practical before there is budget or brand:

- **Direct outbound.** Cold email or LinkedIn outreach to a hand-built list matching the ICP. Slow, but fully controllable and highly informative, since replies and objections are direct feedback.
- **Communities.** Slack groups, forums, subreddits, and industry associations where the ICP already gathers. Participation must be genuine; promotional posting in communities generally backfires.
- **Founder network and warm introductions.** The highest-converting early channel, and the most limited in volume.
- **Content and SEO.** Slow to compound — typically 6 to 12 months before meaningful traffic — but durable once established.
- **Partnerships and integrations.** Effective when another company already reaches your ICP and your product makes theirs more valuable.
- **Paid acquisition.** Generally premature before you know your conversion rate and LTV, since paid channels convert money into learning at an unfavorable rate when the funnel is unproven.

The realistic approach is to test two or three channels seriously rather than sampling many superficially. A channel needs enough volume to produce a signal before you can judge it.



## Design partners

A **design partner** is an early customer who works closely with you on product direction, typically in exchange for a discount, unusual access, or influence over the roadmap. Design partnerships are valuable because they produce deep feedback and a reference customer simultaneously.

Two cautions. First, charge them something, even if discounted; a free partner has no accountability and their feedback is unweighted by cost. Second, avoid building for a single partner's idiosyncratic needs — if three partners want different things, you are learning about the market, but if you build all three you have a consulting business.



## Pricing experiments

Early pricing is a hypothesis, not a commitment. Several practical principles apply.

**Charge from the beginning.** Free products produce unreliable signals about value, and converting a base of free users to paid later is harder than starting paid.

**Anchor on value, not cost.** Price relative to what the customer saves or earns, not what it costs you to deliver. If your product saves a practice 10 hours a month, that quantity frames the conversation.

**Expect to be too cheap initially.** Founders systematically underprice. Raising prices for new customers while grandfathering existing ones is a normal, low-risk experiment; if conversion does not fall, the price was too low.

**Keep the structure simple.** Two or three tiers with a clear reason to move between them is easier to sell than a matrix of features. Complexity in pricing translates directly into friction in the sales conversation.

Test price by quoting different numbers to comparable prospects and observing where hesitation begins. Silence after a number is information; immediate agreement usually means you were low.
