# Revenue Models

A revenue model describes how a business converts value delivered into money received. The choice shapes nearly everything downstream: pricing, sales structure, cash flow timing, and which metrics matter. Changing it later is possible but expensive, because the entire go-to-market organization is built around it.



## B2B versus B2C

**B2B (business-to-business)** companies sell to organizations. Contract values are higher, sales cycles are longer, and the buyer is often not the end user. Annual contract values range from a few hundred dollars for small-business tools to six or seven figures for enterprise software. Sales cycles run from days for low-cost self-serve products to 6 to 18 months for enterprise deals requiring security review, legal negotiation, and procurement approval. B2B revenue is more predictable and churn is generally lower, but each customer requires substantially more effort to acquire.

**B2C (business-to-consumer)** companies sell to individuals. Order values are small, decisions are fast and often emotional, and volume must be very high to reach meaningful revenue. Consumer churn is typically much higher than B2B — monthly churn of 5 to 10 percent is common for consumer subscriptions, versus 1 to 2 percent monthly or lower for B2B SaaS. Consumer businesses win through distribution, virality, and brand rather than through sales teams.

A middle category, sometimes called **B2B2C** or prosumer, sells to individuals who use the product for work — designers, developers, freelancers. These businesses often blend consumer-style self-serve acquisition with B2B-style retention.



## Subscription and SaaS

Subscription models charge recurring fees for continued access. They are dominant in software because they align customer payment with ongoing value delivery and produce predictable revenue.

The core metrics are **MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue)** and **ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue)**, where ARR is generally MRR × 12. Only genuinely recurring revenue counts; one-time setup fees and professional services are excluded.

Healthy SaaS benchmarks worth knowing:

- **Gross margin of 70 to 80 percent** or higher. Software should be cheap to deliver at the margin; materially lower margins suggest heavy infrastructure or hidden services costs.
- **Net revenue retention (NRR) above 100 percent**, meaning existing customers collectively spend more this year than last, after accounting for churn and downgrades. NRR above 120 percent is considered excellent and indicates strong expansion.
- **LTV/CAC ratio of at least 3:1** — the lifetime value of a customer should be roughly three times or more the cost to acquire them.
- **CAC payback under 12 months** for most businesses, meaning gross profit from a customer repays acquisition cost within a year.

Annual prepayment is common and valuable: it improves cash flow, reduces churn, and is usually incentivized with a discount equivalent to one or two months free.



## Freemium and free trials

**Freemium** offers a permanently free tier alongside paid plans. It works when the free tier drives distribution or network effects and when a clear usage or feature boundary triggers upgrade. Free-to-paid conversion rates are typically low — commonly **2 to 5 percent** — which means freemium requires very large top-of-funnel volume to produce meaningful revenue.

**Free trials** offer full access for a limited window, usually 14 or 30 days. Trials convert far better than freemium, often 15 to 25 percent, but generate less organic distribution because non-converting users leave.

The strategic question is whether free users create value beyond potential conversion. If they do — through content, referrals, or network effects — freemium is defensible. If not, it is an expensive marketing channel.



## Usage-based and hybrid pricing

**Usage-based (consumption) pricing** charges according to what the customer actually uses: API calls, storage, compute, messages sent. It aligns cost with value and lowers the barrier to starting, which suits developer tools and infrastructure. The tradeoff is revenue unpredictability for both parties, and customers dislike unexpected bills.

Many companies now use **hybrid models**: a platform fee providing predictable baseline revenue plus usage charges that scale with adoption. This captures the expansion benefit of usage pricing while preserving forecastability.



## Marketplaces and transactional models

**Marketplaces** connect buyers and sellers and take a percentage of each transaction. Take rates commonly fall between **15 and 30 percent**, though they vary enormously — payment-heavy marketplaces operate on a few percent, while marketplaces providing substantial trust, discovery, or logistics value command more.

Marketplaces face the chicken-and-egg problem: neither side joins without the other. The standard approach is to solve one side first, usually supply, often through manual recruitment in a single narrow geography or category before expanding.

**Transactional models** charge per purchase without recurring commitment — e-commerce, one-time software licenses, events. Revenue is lumpy and must be re-earned continually, making customer acquisition cost harder to justify without strong repeat behavior.



## Advertising and other models

**Advertising** monetizes attention rather than the user, measured in CPM (cost per thousand impressions) or CPC (cost per click). It requires very large audiences to generate real revenue and introduces a structural tension between user experience and revenue.

**Licensing** grants use of technology or content for a fee, often to other businesses that embed it in their own products. **Services** revenue — implementation, training, consulting — is common alongside software but carries much lower gross margins, typically 20 to 40 percent, and does not scale with headcount efficiency. Investors discount services revenue heavily when valuing a software company, so the ratio of recurring to services revenue matters for how the business is perceived.
